\section{Conclusion and Limitations}

This paper asked what an ordinary vehicle must acquire to become self-regulating without a coordinator. The answer is a phone the driver already owns, a US\$250 computer and a public traffic feed, which together run a controller that previously needed a central observer and vehicle-level ground truth. The parts list is the result, and not an implementation note. The benefit of a vehicle-side controller grows with the fraction of vehicles carrying it, so the cost of one installation decides whether it ever reaches a road.

Two findings generalize beyond the controller we deployed. The first is that an observation can be queried instead of sensed, because the four quantities a vehicle cannot obtain are why the published design needed a central observer, and a commercial feed supplies a substitute at no cost to the estimated performance. The second is that decentralization then requires no protocol, because a policy that is a deterministic function of a public feed makes vehicles holding the same weights agree by construction. Therefore, the obstacle to a self-regulating fleet is the cost of the first installation and the driver who decides whether to follow the number, rather than the control algorithm.

\noindent\textbf{Limitations.} The chain from a displayed number to a smoother road has one link this paper does not test. We showed that the system computes an advisory on a real road, and not that a driver follows it, for the reasons Section~\ref{sec:intro} gives. The flow-level claim therefore rests on simulation, on a port with its own baseline rather than a reproduction of the published one. Replacing a cloud observer with a commercial feed also exchanges one dependency for another, because a feed carries an unreported lag and terms we do not control. In future work, we aim to look at incentive structures for driver compliance, and other free tier traffic providers for reducing dependency and increasing the robutness of the system.
