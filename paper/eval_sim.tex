\noindent\textbf{Network.} We use the model of the Mainz highway network from~\cite{self_regulating_cars} in SUMO. It has twelve super-segments and ends in an exit where we made three lanes become two, which is where it breaks down. In a simulation a burst appears at the entrances out of nowhere, while on a real road its vehicles arrive along the roads upstream, so we added a 3\,km approach road, split into three super-segments, before each of the eight entrances. The approach roads are a boundary condition that paces the demand into the network, not part of the network, and nothing on them is measured. SUMO inserts every vehicle at the far end of its approach road, earlier by that road's free-flow travel time, so that without control the network receives the same demand at the same times. A vehicle on an approach road is shown the plan of the entrance it feeds and uses its camera as on the network. Controlled vehicles follow the advice through SUMO's speed cap, so car-following still brakes for the vehicle ahead; none of the 1,305 controlled runs had a collision.

\noindent\textbf{Triggers.} Every run starts from a background demand of 3,572\,veh/h, which lies between the flow a jammed exit serves and the flow at which a free one breaks down, so the network does not jam by itself and a jam that forms does not drain (Proposition~\ref{prop:drain}). Fifteen minutes in, one of five triggers is applied. Three are bursts that raise the total inflow to 5,000\,veh/h for ten minutes, or to 7,500 or 9,000\,veh/h for five; they fill the network gradually, and under no control the first jam starts a median of 14 to 28 minutes after the burst begins. Two are stopped vehicles that block a road for five minutes: one lane of the exit road, which jams the exit a median of 80\,s later, and two lanes mid-network, which jams it once the queue reaches the exit, a median of 37 minutes later. Each run lasts two hours, and the feed replays the polled Mainz process with $D=99.5$\,s.

\noindent\textbf{Metrics.} A super-segment is jammed when its mean speed is below 19\,km/h, a threshold fitted to the network's speed distribution by the rule of~\cite{bhardwaj2023understanding}, and a jam starts when that state lasts 120\,s. Jam time is the jammed segment-minutes on the twelve super-segments of the network, and a run is jam-free when the network has no jam. Throughput is the flow served at the exit. Travel time is the vehicle-hours all vehicles spend in the network.

\noindent\textbf{Baselines and protocol.} We compare the global-local advisory of \S~\ref{sec:controller} with two baselines. No control lets every vehicle drive at its own desired speed. The feed lookahead is the controller one would build from the feed alone: it reads the whole network from the same delayed feed and advises 0.5 of the limit when the next super-segment downstream has a jam factor of 2 or more, and 0.75 when the one after it has. All controllers run at full penetration on the same 15 evaluation seeds. Every threshold was chosen on five separate selection seeds, on the network without approach roads, before any evaluation seed ran. We report means over seeds and, for differences, the paired mean with two standard errors.

\subsection{The Camera Warns Before the Feed}
\label{sec:warn}

On the three bursts the camera's density warning came first on 43 of 45 runs, by a median of 210 to 480\,s (Fig.~\ref{fig:warning}). The reason is the shape of the speed-density relation: as occupancy rises to 0.05 the median segment speed falls only from 57.5 to 55.0\,km/h, and then to 35.8\,km/h by 0.07. On the exit block the feed's warning was shorter than $D$ on all 15 runs, so by Proposition~\ref{prop:lag} no controller that reads only the feed could change a speed before that jam began.

\subsection{Jams Prevented and Jam Time}
\label{sec:prevent}

Over the 75 triggered runs no control jammed on 74. The system was jam-free on 51, preventing the jam on 50 runs on which no control jammed, and cut the mean jam time from 130.8 to 4.4 segment-minutes per run, by 97\% (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Fig.~\ref{fig:jams}). It left less jam than no control on all 15 seeds of every trigger, by 69.2 to 195.7 segment-minutes. It prevented every jam after the 5,000 and 7,500\,veh/h bursts, 8 of 15 after the 9,000\,veh/h burst and 12 of 14 after the mid-network block, and after the exit block, which jams within 80\,s, it prevented none but shortened the jam from 137.1 to 8.1 segment-minutes.

Against the feed lookahead the system left 62\% less jam over all triggers ($-7.2\pm1.7$ segment-minutes), and less after four of five triggers, by 5.1 to 10.3 (at most $\pm$2.1). After the largest burst the difference was within noise ($-3.7\pm7.5$): the lookahead was jam-free on 13 of 15 runs against the system's 8, and left 16.3 jammed segment-minutes against the system's 12.6.

\subsection{Throughput and Travel Time}
\label{sec:cost}

Over the triggered runs the system served 2.5\% more traffic than no control ($\pm$0.3\%), between 1.0\% and 4.1\% depending on the trigger, and its vehicles spent 6.0\% less time in the network ($\pm$1.5\%). Travel time in the network was below no control's after the 7,500 and 9,000\,veh/h bursts and the exit block, by 6.9\% to 11.0\%, and within noise after the 5,000\,veh/h burst and the mid-network block.

The lookahead served 5.4\% less traffic than no control and added 11.9\% to its travel time in the network, so the system served 8.3\% more traffic than the lookahead and spent 16.0\% less travel time in the network. The difference is what each one slows. The lookahead slows every vehicle upstream of a segment the feed reports as slowing, whether or not that vehicle's own road is filling. The local layer slows only the vehicles whose road ahead is filling, by one step, and changed the advised speed on 17\% of vehicle ticks.

\noindent\textbf{Camera distance scale.} We repeated the largest burst and the exit block with every camera distance scaled by 0.8 and by 1.25, since the absolute scale is assumed (\S~\ref{sec:road}). On the exit block it still left 9.2 to 10.6 fewer jammed segment-minutes than the lookahead. On the burst it left 12.9 fewer at 1.25 ($\pm$6.0), but at 0.8, which reads the road as fuller and changed the advised speed on 24\% of vehicle ticks instead of 19\%, it left 25.0 more ($\pm$15.9).

\noindent\textbf{Share of drivers who follow.} We varied the share of vehicles that follow the advice from 50\% to 100\% in steps of 5\% on the largest burst and the exit block (Fig.~\ref{fig:penetration}). On the exit block the system left less jam than no control at every share. On the burst it did so from 55\%, by 50.4 segment-minutes ($\pm$34.2), but with 50\% following it left 29.5 more ($\pm$24.6). The excess at 50\% comes from the local layer: the plan alone left 19.6 fewer than no control there ($\pm$21.0), and adding the correction raised the jam by 49.1 ($\pm$20.2). With half the vehicles following, the jam formed on every run, and the correction then slowed the following vehicles in the queue before the exit, so the exit served 100\,veh/h less than under the plan alone. On the burst the correction reduced the jam below the plan alone's from 65\% ($-29.4\pm25.5$), and from 60\% with the feed gate ($-42.9\pm31.7$). From 80\% on, more followers did not reduce the jam further: the system's jam time on the burst stayed between 7.4 and 12.6 segment-minutes, and it was jam-free on 8 to 11 of 15 runs, so four in five vehicles following gave the result of full penetration.

\noindent\textbf{Feed delay.} We varied the feed delay $D$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:lag}): 0, 99.5, 189 and 368\,s, the last close to the probe latency of~\cite{wang2018latency} plus the measured delivery lag. On the exit block, which jams faster than the feed reports, both controllers left more jam at the longest delay than with none, and the system stayed below the lookahead at every delay. On the largest burst the system left less jam than the lookahead at 0, 189 and 368\,s, by 6.6 to 12.7 segment-minutes (at most $\pm$8.7), and the same at the measured delay ($-3.7\pm7.5$); neither controller's jam time there rose or fell steadily with $D$.

\subsection{When No Jam Would Have Formed}
\label{sec:notrigger}

The local layer cannot tell whether a jam would have formed, so it also slows vehicles that did not need slowing. On runs with the background demand and no trigger, where no controller jams, the correction changed the advised speed on 15\% of vehicle ticks, which reduced throughput by 6.9\% relative to no control and added 23.2\% to the travel time in the network; the lookahead reduced throughput by 20.8\%.

Most of this cost goes away if the local layer acts only while the feed reports the network near capacity. We tested such a feed gate: a vehicle's correction may switch on only while its feed version shows a jam factor of at least 0.5 on some super-segment, measured against one step below the plan's speed so that the advisory's own slowing does not hold the gate open. With no trigger the gated correction changed the advised speed on 1.0\% of vehicle ticks and reduced throughput by 3.4\%, close to the plan alone's 2.9\%. After the triggers it was jam-free on 56 of 75 runs, against 51 without the gate, but because it waits for the delayed feed it left more jam after the exit block ($+3.6\pm1.1$ segment-minutes) and the mid-network block ($+1.9\pm0.8$). On the selection seeds the gated layer reduced jam time less than the ungated one, so the results above are for the ungated layer.
