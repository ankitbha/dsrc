\noindent\textbf{Network.} We use the model of the Mainz highway network from~\cite{self_regulating_cars} in SUMO. It has twelve super-segments and ends in an exit where we made three lanes become two, which is where it breaks down. In a simulation a burst appears at the entrances out of nowhere, while on a real road its vehicles arrive along the roads upstream, so we added a 3\,km approach road before each of the eight entrances. The approach roads pace the demand into the network, and nothing on them is measured. Controlled vehicles follow the advice through SUMO's speed cap, so car-following still brakes for the vehicle ahead; none of the 3,000 controlled runs had a collision.

\noindent\textbf{Triggers.} Every run starts from a background demand of 3,572\,veh/h, which lies between the flow a jammed exit serves and the flow at which a free one breaks down, so the network does not jam by itself and a jam that forms does not drain (Proposition~\ref{prop:drain}). Fifteen minutes in, one of five triggers is applied. Three are bursts that raise the total inflow to 5,000\,veh/h for ten minutes, or to 7,500 or 9,000\,veh/h for five; under no control the first jam starts a median of 14 to 28 minutes after the burst begins. Two are stopped vehicles that block a road for five minutes: one lane of the exit road, which jams the exit a median of 80\,s later, and two lanes mid-network, which jams it a median of 37 minutes later. Each run lasts two hours, and the feed replays the polled Mainz process with $D=99.5$\,s.

\noindent\textbf{Metrics.} A super-segment is jammed when its mean speed is below 19\,km/h, a threshold fitted to the network's speed distribution by the rule of~\cite{bhardwaj2023understanding}, and a jam starts when that state lasts 120\,s. Jam time is the jammed segment-minutes on the twelve super-segments of the network, and a run is jam-free when the network has no jam. Throughput is the flow served at the exit. Travel time is the vehicle-hours all vehicles spend in the network.

\noindent\textbf{Baselines, ablations and protocol.} No control lets every vehicle drive at its own desired speed. The feed lookahead is the controller one would build from the feed alone: it advises 0.5 of the limit when the next super-segment downstream has a jam factor of 2 or more, and 0.75 when the one after it has. The ablations each remove one part: the camera (plan only), the plan and the feed (camera only, on a plan of 1.0), the gate, and the camera's error (true occupancy in its 60\,m window). All run at full penetration on the same 15 evaluation seeds. Thresholds were chosen on five separate selection seeds, the gate last, as the candidate with the least jam among those costing at most 1\% of throughput beyond the plan alone with no trigger. We report means over seeds and, for differences, the paired mean with two standard errors.

\subsection{The Camera Warns Before the Feed}
\label{sec:warn}

On the three bursts the camera's density warning came first on 43 of 45 runs, by a median of 210 to 480\,s (Fig.~\ref{fig:warning}). The reason is the shape of the speed-density relation: as occupancy rises to 0.05 the median segment speed falls only from 57.5 to 55.0\,km/h, and then to 35.8\,km/h by 0.07. On the exit block the feed's warning was shorter than $D$ on all 15 runs, so by Proposition~\ref{prop:lag} no controller that reads only the feed could change a speed before that jam began.

\subsection{Jams Prevented and Jam Time}
\label{sec:prevent}

No control jammed on 74 of the 75 triggered runs. The system was jam-free on 56, preventing 55 of those jams, and cut the mean jam time from 130.8 to 3.9 segment-minutes per run, by 97\% (Table~\ref{tab:main}, Fig.~\ref{fig:jams}). After the exit block, which jams within 80\,s, it prevented no jam but shortened it from 137.1 to 11.1 segment-minutes. Against the feed lookahead it left 67\% less jam ($-7.8\pm1.3$ segment-minutes), less after every trigger, and served 9.9\% more traffic.

\subsection{What Each Layer Contributes}
\label{sec:ablation}

Neither layer prevents the jams alone (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The plan alone was jam-free on 39 runs and on none after the largest burst, which the feed reports late. The camera alone was jam-free on 5, although it changed the shown speed on 32\% of vehicle-ticks: from a plan of 1.0 its one step reaches only 0.75 of the limit. Together they were jam-free on 51 runs without the gate and 56 with it, and true occupancy in place of the camera added two more. The system served 3.9\% more traffic than no control ($\pm$0.3\%) with 12.6\% less time in the network ($\pm$1.4\%); the lookahead served 5.4\% less, because it slows every vehicle upstream of a segment the feed reports as slowing, whether or not its own road is filling.

\subsection{The Cost When No Jam Would Form}
\label{sec:notrigger}

Near capacity, ordinary traffic also brings the occupancy ahead close to $k_c$. With no trigger, where no controller jams, the layers without the gate served 6.9\% less traffic than no control, against the plan alone's 2.9\%, and 5.9\% less on true occupancy, so the loss comes mostly from the rule rather than from the camera's error. The gate cut it to 3.0\%, and the share of vehicle-ticks with a corrected speed from 15.5\% to 0.2\%. Letting the camera also open the gate, at $\hat k\ge0.8k_c$ or $k_c$, exceeded the 1\% bound on the selection seeds, and releasing the correction while the road ahead discharges changed jam by less than the tie margin. The gate's price is the exit block, which jams before the feed opens the gate: $3.0\pm0.9$ more segment-minutes than without it.

\subsection{Partial Compliance and Driver Response}
\label{sec:sens}

\noindent\textbf{Share of drivers who follow.} On the largest burst and the exit block (Fig.~\ref{fig:penetration}), the system left less jam than no control at every share on the exit block and from 55\% on the burst, and at 75\% it left 18.5 segment-minutes after the burst against 41.4 without the gate. At 50\% on the burst it left $13.0\pm15.9$ more than no control, against $29.5\pm24.6$ without the gate, and $32.6\pm17.2$ more than the plan alone; releasing the correction in a discharging queue did not reduce this ($+13.1\pm21.3$).

\noindent\textbf{Driver response.} We placed a driver between the display and the speed cap, who reacts to a change after a lognormal reaction time, aims above the advice by a compliance offset, and slows toward a lower advice at a bounded deceleration. The parameters are assumed, since no driver acted on an advisory on the drives: a median of 2\,s, $5\pm5\%$ and 1.5\,m/s$^2$ (nominal), and 4\,s, $15\pm10\%$ and 1.0\,m/s$^2$ (lax). The system then left 11.1 and 46.7 segment-minutes after the burst (3.7 with exact compliance) and 12.3 and 13.7 after the exit block (11.1), less than no control on every seed and than the plan alone under the same drivers.

\noindent\textbf{Camera scale and feed delay.} With camera distances scaled by 0.8 and 1.25 (\S~\ref{sec:road}), the system's jam changed by at most 1.8 segment-minutes and stayed below the lookahead's; without the gate the burst at 0.8 left 25.0 more than the lookahead ($\pm$15.9). With $D$ of 0, 189 and 368\,s it left less jam than the lookahead on the exit block at every delay and on the burst at 0\,s. At 189 and 368\,s on the burst the two were within noise ($+2.9\pm10.8$, $-0.8\pm5.3$), because the gate also waits for the feed: the layers left 4.9 and 6.8 segment-minutes without it, 14.5 and 17.5 with it.
