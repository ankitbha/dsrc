\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A free-flow road network can jam without a crash and without a fixed bottleneck.
A small burst of inflow pushes local density past a critical value, flow on the
affected links falls, and the queue then feeds itself until the demand drains
away~\cite{bhardwaj2023understanding,sugiyama2008traffic}. The fundamental
diagram states the same fact as a curve: flow rises with density to a critical
point and falls after it~\cite{greenshields1935study,lighthill1955kinematic,richards1956shock}.
A controller that holds density below that point therefore protects
throughput, and it does not need to know anything about the rest of the network
to do so.

Earlier work introducing self-regulating cars built such a controller and
measured it. We divided the Mainz highway network into twelve
super-segments, learned a policy that assigns each super-segment a
desired speed, and applied that speed to the vehicles on it. In PTV Vissim the
policy raised throughput by 5\% and cut average delay by 13\% against no
control~\cite{self_regulating_cars}. The result holds at partial adoption and
improves as the controlled fraction grows. Its deployment model is centralized.
A central coordinator collects the network state, computes the action, and
sends every participating vehicle its desired speed.

This work is not the first to propose a vehicle-side traffic controller.
Learned controllers that smooth mixed-autonomy
traffic have been studied for years, and they report gains at low penetration
in microscopic
simulators~\cite{wu2017flow,vinitsky2018benchmarks,kreidieh2018dissipating}.
Deployment, on the other hand, is generally treated
as an engineering detail rather than as a research question, and what it would
cost goes unreported, except for a few exceptions~\cite{stern2018dissipation,lee2024traffic}.

This paper tackles deploying self-regulating cars as its central question.
Two properties of the centralized model make its deployment costly. The first is the
observation. The policy reads six features per super-segment, and four of them
come from vehicle-level ground truth that a vehicle cannot easily obtain in reality. 
The second property is the
cloud coordinator. It has to be built, paid for, kept available, and trusted,
and it sits inside the control loop.

Ease of adoption is decided by the size of the change. 
The controller's benefit grows with the fraction of vehicles carrying
it. This fraction is therefore the quantity a deployment design has to
maximise. Anything that raises the cost of one installation lowers it. A
purpose-built vehicle~\cite{lee2024traffic}, a roadside
sensor~\cite{arnold1998ramp,papageorgiou2002freeway} and a per-vehicle service
contract~\cite{self_regulating_cars} each put a floor under that cost, and
each has been part of a previous design. The target here is the opposite. It
is the least that has to be added to a vehicle that already exists.
We call this target the minimum viable deployment, and discuss it in 
Section~\ref{sec:vision}. The answer has four parts: an
ordinary Android phone, one single-board computer, a traffic API, and a filter
that bounds the advisory before it is displayed. The computer is an NVIDIA
Jetson Orin Nano developer kit, which lists at about
US\$250~\cite{nvidia2025orinnano}. The phone used for the drives reported here
is a OnePlus Nord N10 (2021 model), which is a mid-range handset rather than a flagship.
Neither part is purpose-built, nothing is installed on the road, and no server
has to be hosted. 

The division of labour inside the rig is as follows: the
phone captures and forwards, the Jetson interprets and decides. Nothing is
wired to the throttle, the brakes, the steering or the vehicle bus. The only
connection to the vehicle is a 12\,V power lead, so the installation changes
no system the vehicle already depends on. 

\noindent\textbf{Scope of the work.}
No advisory this project computed has ever been acted on. On every drive, the
human drove without interfere while the Jetson rendered a
recommended speed on the phone's display. There are two independent
reasons for this. An experiment in which an advisory
influences a human driver requires approval from an institutional review board,
which this project does not hold. One instrumented vehicle also cannot produce a
flow effect, so a compliance experiment on a single car would establish nothing
about throughput or delay. In this paper ``deployed'' means that the system ran
on a road and computed its outputs there. It does not mean that the recommendations 
were acted on. The flow-level claim is made in simulation for the same reason.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A vehicle-side realization of the self-regulating-cars controller, built
from commodity parts, with a sensing scheduler that sets the sampling rate of every sensor at run
time rather than fixing it at install time (Sections~\ref{sec:vision}
and~\ref{sec:architecture}).
\item A measured account of that system running on a road: eight drives with a total 
of 152.8 minutes on vehicle power, with per-stage timing and
field provenance recorded (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item The experiences that deployment produced, reported in the same section.
The phone overheated on the road, from its own load and from sunlight on the
windscreen, and a ninety-degree camera rotation sent perception to offline
processing for the whole campaign.
\item A flow-level measurement of the observation change. On a SUMO port of the
same Mainz network, a policy restricted to the five fields a traffic API returns
gains $230 \pm 44$\,veh/h over no control, and the original six-feature policy
gains $224 \pm 61$\,veh/h (Section~\ref{sec:simulation}).
\end{itemize}
