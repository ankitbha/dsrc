A highway that carries traffic close to its capacity can jam without a crash or a fixed bottleneck. A short burst of inflow, or a vehicle stopped in one lane for a few minutes, pushes the local density past its critical value. The outflow of the road then falls, and the queue sustains itself, spiralling into a suddent traffic jam~\cite{bhardwaj2023understanding}. Such a jam also takes significantly longer to drain.
We simulated such conditions on the Mainz road network~\cite{self_regulating_cars} by blocking a lane at the exit for five minutes, and found that it increases the network's total time under jam to 137 segment-minutes. A jammed road near capacity serves less traffic than keeps arriving and the queue does not drain (Section~\ref{sec:feed}). 
Such a jam has to be prevented rather than cleared: the warning, and the intervention, must come before the road breaks down.

Existing controllers obtain that warning from infrastructure. Variable speed limits and ramp meters hold a bottleneck below breakdown using loop detectors and overhead gantries~\cite{hegyi2008specialist,papageorgiou2002freeway}, and vehicle-based controllers send target speeds to equipped vehicles from a central planner that observes the traffic~\cite{lee2024traffic}. Most roads have neither the roadside equipment nor the coordinator. One important concern for such approaches is the cost to deployment. Installing and maintaining infrastructure costs public funds, and a central controller has maintanence and privacy costs. Adoption is the limiting factor in both cases, since adoption dictates how much public benefit gets derived from these technologies.

Our system was designed with this deployment cost and adoption in mind, with a minimum set of changes to an ordinary vehicle that can enable this advisory. We consider two sources of information: a commercial traffic probe feed for every segment of the network, and a dashboard camera. The feed is global but aggregate and late: it reports segment speeds, which barely change until a road is about to break down, commercial feeds report a change in speed a mean of 4.4 minutes after it happens~\cite{wang2018latency}, and the feed we use publishes once a minute and becomes readable 29 to 90\,s after each publication. The camera is immediate but local: from the gaps to the vehicles ahead it measures density, which rises before speed falls, but only over the next 60\,m. Thus, each modality covers what the other misses.

This paper presents a speed advisory system built on that complementarity (Fig.~\ref{fig:overview}). An onboard computer in each vehicle runs two layers. The \emph{global layer} turns each published version of the feed into a speed plan for the whole network; the plan is a deterministic function of the version, and every vehicle switches to it at a time the version itself sets, so all vehicles show the same plan without exchanging a message. The \emph{local layer} estimates the density of the road ahead from the camera every 5\,s and lowers its own vehicle's advised speed by one step below the plan when that density is about to reach its critical value. The driver sees one number: the advised speed for the current road segment.

We built the system from a phone and Jetson Nano, a US\$250 onboard computer, and deployed it in a car on public roads in central New Jersey. We then built the simulated camera from the frames recorded on those drives. In simulation on the Mainz highway network, extended by 3\,km approach roads so that queues pushed upstream are counted, the system prevented the jam in 40 of 75 triggered runs, cut jam time from 131.7 to 16.0 segment-minutes per run, and served 2.5\% more traffic than no control at the cost of 1.6\% more total delay; a controller reading the same feed without the camera left 22.5 segment-minutes of jam and served 5.4\% less traffic.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.} We make four contributions.
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item We design a global-local advisory architecture in which vehicles agree on a network-wide plan by reading the same feed version, with no messages between them and no server computing their speeds, and each vehicle's camera corrects the plan by at most one step (Sections~\ref{sec:system},~\ref{sec:controller}).
\item We measure the feed's delivery process and analyse the timing of feed-only control: we derive a lag condition under which no feed-only controller can act before a jam, and a sampled-margin bound that sets the local layer's horizon (Section~\ref{sec:feed}).
\item We deploy the system on US\$500 of commodity hardware, report its latency and failure modes, and build a camera error model from 18,818 road frames (Section~\ref{sec:road}).
\item We evaluate the system over five jam triggers against no control and a feed-only lookahead, and test its sensitivity to camera scale, compliance and feed delay (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\end{itemize}
