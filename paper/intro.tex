An ordinary car cannot take part in traffic control. It has no channel to a
traffic authority, no sensor that reports anything beyond its own bumper, and no
way to be told what speed would help the road it is on. Proposals that give it
one have assumed a purpose-built vehicle, a roadside installation, or a service
contract per car. This paper asks what the cheapest sufficient addition is,
builds it, and drives it. The answer is a phone the driver already owns and a
US\$250 single-board computer, with nothing installed on the road and no server
inside the control loop (Figure~\ref{fig:teaser}).

The traffic failure this addresses is specific. A free-flow network can jam
without a crash and without a fixed bottleneck: a small burst of inflow pushes
local density past a critical value, flow on the affected links falls, and the
queue feeds itself until the demand drains
away~\cite{bhardwaj2023understanding,sugiyama2008traffic}. The fundamental
diagram states the same fact as a curve, with flow rising with density to a
critical point and falling after
it~\cite{greenshields1935study,lighthill1955kinematic,richards1956shock}. A
controller that holds density below that point therefore protects throughput,
without knowing anything about the rest of the network.

Earlier work introducing self-regulating cars built such a controller and
measured it. That work divided the Mainz highway network into twelve
super-segments, learned a policy that assigns each super-segment a desired
speed, and applied that speed to the vehicles on it. In PTV Vissim the policy
raised throughput by 5\% and cut average delay by 13\% against no
control~\cite{self_regulating_cars}. The result holds at partial adoption and
improves as the controlled fraction grows. Its deployment model is centralized.
A central coordinator collects the network state, computes the action, and
sends every participating vehicle its desired speed.

This work is not the first to propose a vehicle-side traffic controller.
Learned controllers that smooth mixed-autonomy traffic have been studied for
years, and they report gains at low penetration in microscopic
simulators~\cite{wu2017flow,vinitsky2018benchmarks,kreidieh2018dissipating}.
Deployment, on the other hand, is generally treated as an engineering detail
rather than as a research question, and what it would cost goes unreported,
except for a few exceptions~\cite{stern2018dissipation,lee2024traffic}.

This paper tackles deploying self-regulating cars as its central question.
Two properties of the centralized model make its deployment costly. The first is
the observation: the policy reads six features per super-segment, and four of
them are vehicle-level ground truth a vehicle cannot easily obtain. The second
is the cloud coordinator, which has to be built, paid for, kept available and
trusted, and which sits inside the control loop.

Ease of adoption is decided by the size of the change, because the controller's
benefit grows with the fraction of vehicles carrying it. That fraction is
therefore the quantity a deployment design has to maximise, and anything raising
the cost of one installation lowers it. A purpose-built
vehicle~\cite{lee2024traffic}, a roadside
sensor~\cite{arnold1998ramp,papageorgiou2002freeway} and a per-vehicle service
contract~\cite{self_regulating_cars} each put a floor under that cost. The
target here is the opposite: the least that has to be added to a vehicle that
already exists. We call this the minimum viable deployment and discuss it in
Section~\ref{sec:vision}. Table~\ref{tab:bom} is the resulting parts list. The
computer is an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano developer kit, listing at about
US\$250~\cite{nvidia2025orinnano}, and the phone used for the drives reported
here is a OnePlus Nord N10 (2021 model), a mid-range handset rather than a
flagship.

The division of labour inside the rig is as follows: the
phone captures and forwards, the Jetson interprets and decides. Nothing is
wired to the throttle, the brakes, the steering or the vehicle bus. The only
connection to the vehicle is a 12\,V power lead, so the installation changes
no system the vehicle already depends on.

\noindent\textbf{The driver is the actuator, through an interface that already
exists.}
This system sets no speed. It displays one, and a person decides what to do
about it. That is the arrangement a navigation application already runs: a phone
on a windscreen mount, showing a number derived from a traffic estimate the
phone did not measure itself, which the driver follows or ignores. Mobile
telematics platforms operate the same driver-facing rail at the scale of tens of
millions of drivers~\cite{cmt2025,samsara2025}. What changes here is which
number is shown and what it is computed from, not how it reaches the driver and
not what authority it has. That supports a claim about deployability, namely
that the interface needs no new hardware, no new habit and no regulatory change.
It says nothing about what fraction of drivers would comply.

\noindent\textbf{Scope of the work.}
No advisory this project computed has ever been acted on. On every drive, the
human drove without interfere while the Jetson rendered a
recommended speed on the phone's display. There are two independent
reasons for this. An experiment in which an advisory
influences a human driver requires approval from an institutional review board,
which this project does not hold. One instrumented vehicle also cannot produce a
flow effect, so a compliance experiment on a single car would establish nothing
about throughput or delay. In this paper ``deployed'' means that the system ran
on a road and computed its outputs there. It does not mean that the recommendations
were acted on. The flow-level claim is made in simulation for the same reason.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A vehicle-side realization of the self-regulating-cars controller from
commodity parts, with a sensing scheduler that sets every sensor's sampling rate
at run time rather than at install time (Sections~\ref{sec:vision}
and~\ref{sec:architecture}).
\item A measured account of it running on a road: eight drives, 152.8 minutes on
vehicle power, with per-stage timing and field provenance recorded, and the
deployment experiences that produced. The phone overheated from its own load and
from sunlight on the windscreen, and a ninety-degree camera rotation sent
perception to offline processing for the whole campaign (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item A flow-level measurement of the observation change. On a SUMO port of the
same Mainz network, a policy restricted to the five fields a traffic API returns
gains $230 \pm 44$\,veh/h over no control, and the original six-feature policy
gains $224 \pm 61$\,veh/h (Section~\ref{sec:simulation}).
\end{itemize}
