\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

A free-flow road network can jam without a crash and without a fixed bottleneck.
A small burst of inflow pushes local density past a critical value, flow on the
affected links falls, and the queue then feeds itself until the demand drains
away~\cite{bhardwaj2023understanding,sugiyama2008traffic}. The fundamental
diagram states the same fact as a curve: flow rises with density to a critical
point and falls after it~\cite{greenshields1935study,lighthill1955kinematic,richards1956shock}.
A controller that holds density below that point therefore protects
throughput, and it does not need to know anything about the rest of the network
to do so.

Earlier work built such a controller and measured it. Self-regulating cars
divide a highway network into twelve super-segments of two to three kilometres,
learn a policy that assigns each super-segment a desired speed, and apply that
speed to the vehicles on it. On a Mainz highway network in PTV Vissim the policy
raised throughput by 5\% and cut average delay by 13\% against no
control~\cite{self_regulating_cars}. The result holds at partial adoption and
improves as the controlled fraction grows. Its deployment model is centralized.
A central observer collects the network state, a cloud service computes the
action, and every participating vehicle receives its desired speed over an
internet connection.

Two properties of that model decide what a deployment costs. The first is the
observation. The policy reads six features per super-segment, and four of them
come from vehicle-level ground truth that a vehicle cannot obtain. The published
implementation computes the mean gap from each vehicle's follow distance, and
computes entry and exit rates as set differences over the vehicle identities
present one minute apart. A vehicle has none of that. The second property is the
cloud coordinator. It has to be built, paid for, kept available, and trusted,
and it sits inside the control loop.

This paper is about removing both. It asks what has to exist on an ordinary
vehicle for that controller to run on the vehicle, and it reports what happened
when the answer was built and driven. The answer is an unmodified Android phone,
an NVIDIA Jetson Orin Nano, a traffic API, and a filter that bounds the advisory
before it is displayed. The phone captures and forwards. The Jetson interprets
and decides. Nothing is wired to the throttle, the brakes, the steering or the
vehicle bus.

\noindent\textbf{Scope, stated before any result.}
No advisory this project computed has ever been acted on. On every drive the
human drove and the software did not interfere. The Jetson rendered a
recommended speed on its own display, nobody was asked to follow it, and no
measurement here depends on anyone having followed it. There are two independent
reasons, and either one is sufficient. An experiment in which an advisory
influences a human driver requires approval from an institutional review board,
which this project does not hold. One instrumented vehicle also cannot produce a
flow effect, so a compliance experiment on a single car would establish nothing
about throughput or delay. In this paper ``deployed'' means that the system ran
on a road and computed its outputs there. It does not mean that anything acted
on them. The flow-level claim is made in simulation for the same reason, and the
drives are never asked to support it.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.2em,itemsep=1pt,topsep=2pt]
\item A vehicle-side realization of the self-regulating-cars controller, built
from commodity parts, with the sampling rate of every sensor set at run time by
the compute device rather than fixed at install time (Sections~\ref{sec:vision}
and~\ref{sec:architecture}).
\item A measured account of that system running on a road: eight drives,
22{,}929 ticks and 152.8 minutes on vehicle power, with per-stage timing and
field provenance recorded per tick (Section~\ref{sec:eval}).
\item The failures the deployment produced, reported as results
(Section~\ref{sec:experience}). On the thermal task alone, five distinct false
readings came from measurement harnesses rather than from the code under test.
One further defect was a ninety-degree frame rotation, which accounts for every
zero-detection drive in the project.
\item A flow-level measurement of the observation change. On a SUMO port of the
same Mainz network, a policy restricted to the five fields a traffic API returns
gains $230 \pm 44$\,veh/h over no control, and the original six-feature policy
gains $224 \pm 61$\,veh/h (Section~\ref{sec:simulation}).
\end{itemize}
