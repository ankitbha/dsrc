This section states the minimum a vehicle has to acquire for the self-regulating
cars vision to be deployed on it, and Figure~\ref{fig:overview} is the loop that
results.

\noindent\textbf{Two sensing layers, and two observation spaces.}
The deployment carries two decisions, and each reads its own sensor. The
advisory layer reads a traffic API per super-segment and decides a desired
speed. The safety layer reads the camera, GPS and inertial unit and decides
whether that speed may be shown. Neither sensor can supply the other layer's
inputs, which is the reason for the split. A traffic API reports aggregate
quantities per road segment and contains no individual vehicles, so it cannot
report a leader distance or a follower gap. A forward camera is the opposite: it
measures the vehicle ahead and reports nothing beyond its own horizon.

\noindent\textbf{The advisory observation space.}
The advisory layer reads the whole network at once, as a fixed partition into
super-segments with five fields each. Three come from the traffic feed, which in
this deployment is the HERE Traffic API~\cite{here_traffic_api}, and two from the
map. \texttt{speed} is the segment's current aggregate speed, the quantity being
regulated, and \texttt{free\_flow} is the speed it carries when uncongested, so
the two together state how far below its own capacity the segment is running.
\texttt{jam\_factor} is a 0 to 10 congestion index summarising the same shortfall
on a bounded scale the feed publishes directly. \texttt{lanes} turns a
per-segment reading into a per-lane one, and \texttt{length\_km} says how much
road that reading describes. The feed's \texttt{confidence} and
\texttt{traversability} are not read.

The action is one of three speed fractions per super-segment. The base it
multiplies is that segment's static speed limit from the map, deliberately not
the feed's free-flow reading, because a live reading used for the base as well
as for the observation would enter the loop twice. The driver sees one number,
the row for the super-segment the vehicle's position currently falls on.

\noindent\textbf{The safety observation space.}
The safety layer reads the road immediately around the vehicle. The forward
camera supplies the distance to the vehicle ahead and the rate at which that
distance is closing. GPS supplies ego speed, and the slope of that speed over a
short window supplies ego acceleration. The tracked vehicles in range supply a
local density and a local mean speed. Those are the quantities needed to decide
that a recommended speed is unsafe or obstructive at this moment, which is a
judgement about the road inside the camera's range rather than about the
network.

\noindent\textbf{Why not aggregate from local sensing.}
Another design could reconstruct segment-level state from a vehicle's own
sensors and those of its neighbours. That needs either vehicle-to-vehicle
exchange or a server that aggregates, and the second is exactly the deployment
cost we want to avoid. Local sensing also needs a sufficient number of compliant
vehicles before it can produce a rich segment state, so it cannot be tested with
a unit deployment at all. Here the per-vehicle quantities stay where they are
measurable, and they feed the safety layer only.

\noindent\textbf{Decentralized execution.}
The policy's input is the whole network's state, and the policy is a
deterministic argmax over three speed fractions. Every vehicle holding the same
weights, reading the same map and querying the same feed computes the same
action for the same super-segment, so decentralized execution reproduces
centralized execution by construction. What differs on a road is staleness: a
response carries the time it was received, which this system measures, and not
the time at which the conditions it describes held. The query is small, at
O(100) numbers per decision on a 60\,s interval, and one request by corridor or
circle covers the whole extent.

\noindent\textbf{What the vehicle must therefore carry.}
Five things. (1) A network definition that names the super-segments and their
edges. (2) A traffic-feed query bounded by that network's extent. (3) A policy
trained on that network. (4) A local sensor set for the safety layer. (5) A
display. There is no connection to the throttle, the brakes, the steering or the
vehicle bus, and no server inside the control loop.
